## Project description

*What She Left Out* is a short, story-driven narrative game (30–60 minutes) set in a humid urban village in Guangzhou around 2016, a neighborhood that is about to be demolished.

The protagonist is a young woman in her twenties who teaches math at a small tutoring center, a job she never chose. She once wanted to study philosophy and become a novelist, but under her family's financial pressure and her mother's expectations, she chose the "practical" path. Every morning she wakes up having forgotten something more: not her name or her job, but her old dreams and the person she wanted to be. **[TODO: 失忆是"只忘梦想"还是"忘记一切"，还没定；这里先按你最新的笔记写成"只忘梦想"]**

The player is a ghost-like presence who cannot speak to her. By day, the player guides her through her routine, but she can only act on what she knows today. At night, while she sleeps, the player can leave traces for her to find in the morning: circling words in the newspaper, cutting words out to form messages, or writing directly. But a ghost is not strong enough to use anything she put away, so what the player can "say" each night depends on what she left out during the day. The newspaper changes every day, the pen is sometimes taken away, and in the wet "return-south" weather, paper softens and ink runs.

The surface of the game has clear rules and answers: what she can do, what she remembers, how far she can go. Underneath, the game asks questions that have no single answer: what you let her remember shapes who she becomes, and in the end the player must decide whether she should remember everything.

The game will be built in Unity, with dialogue written in Yarn Spinner.

## Central question

**What happens to a person who keeps chasing one absolute answer, in a life that is slowly fading into fog?**

More concretely for the game: *Can a game let players feel the gap between wanting certainty and living with vagueness, by giving them a surface that has clear answers and a core that refuses to give one?*

The project addresses this through its structure:

- **The surface has answers.** Each day has concrete goals and rules. What the protagonist knows determines what she can do, and the player can see progress: remember a little more, get a little further. Like the murder case in *Disco Elysium* or the investigation in *Silver Pines Town*, the surface is logical, so the player always knows what to do.
- **The core has no single answer.** The traces are fragments; the protagonist has to guess what they mean, and she may not believe them. Who the ghost is, and why she forgets, are left open to several readings, while I, as the author, hold my own preference, as Robert Kurvitz describes for *Disco Elysium*.
- **Choice is control.** The player can only leave a few traces each night, so every night they choose what she will remember. The game asks what it means to shape another person's self, and whether doing it "for her own good" is any different from her mother's expectations.

## Motivation

At the start of this semester my thesis was about accessibility and alt text. I chose it because I had people to interview and because it felt practical for finding a job. While researching it, I realized I wasn't really interested, and that much of what I had made before was what I felt I *should* make. I started over.

I wanted to make something that actually comes from me. I dug into what I am drawn to (games like *Disco Elysium* and *Cyberpunk 2077*, literature about failure and split selves) and chose my medium: a complete, story-driven game, much smaller than the works I love.

To find the content, I wrote down nine real moments from my life (notebook pages 71–78) and mapped them by hand. Almost every entry had the same shape: *"I always thought I was X; it turned out I was Y."* Under all of them was one contradiction: **wanting the absolute, while everything fades into fog.** Two threads kept returning: other people's expectations (my mother's investment in me, the old story of the fallen prodigy Fang Zhongyong, the belief that "mediocrity is a sin") and the feeling that since college my life has been foggy, faded, nearsighted.

These are things I struggle with, and many people around me struggle with too, especially people who grew up in East Asian families where expectations are a form of love and control at the same time. I don't want to write my own story directly. I want to write an original story that holds these questions, specific enough that someone who hasn't lived it can still recognize something.

## Intended audience

**Who it is for.** Players who enjoy text-heavy, character-driven narrative games, such as *Disco Elysium*, *Night in the Woods*, *Kentucky Route Zero* and *Citizen Sleeper*. Within that group, I especially have in mind people in their twenties who grew up measured against other people's expectations and now feel unsure what they want, many of them, but not only, from East Asian families.

**Why it matters to them.** Games about mental states often explain them. I want to give players a concrete situation instead: a person, a neighborhood, a set of rules. Recognition comes from specific details (a damp room, a circled word, a mother who tidies away your pen), not from a universal statement about "being lost." For players from outside this context, the game offers a window into a very specific place and time: a Guangzhou urban village before demolition.

**Where they will encounter it.** A playable build on itch.io, the ITP Thesis Show in May, and playtest sessions during the semester.

**How I will engage them during development.**
- Paper prototypes before code: one person plays the protagonist and only sees what is on the desk each "morning"; I play the ghost with a limited number of words per night.
- Regular playtests with classmates at each critique, plus remote playtests with friends in China, to check whether the setting reads to both audiences.
- Feedback from ITP alumni (Thesis Alumni Chats on Nov 18, including Alexander Porter, whose work uses game engines to tell stories about place and memory).

Fisher, Kierkegaard, Han and Xiang Biao give language to the themes: lost futures, not wanting to be oneself, self-exploitation, and expectations in a specifically Chinese context.

## Form

**Medium.** A 2D narrative game for desktop, built in Unity with Yarn Spinner for dialogue. Estimated scope: 30–60 minutes of play, one main location (a few rooms and alleys in one urban village), 3–5 characters, 2–4 inner voices, roughly 15,000–30,000 words of dialogue.

**Core loop.**
1. **Day:** the player guides the protagonist through her routine. She can only act on what she knows today; options she doesn't know about are greyed out.
2. **Leaving things out:** what she leaves on her desk (the day's newspaper, a pen, a book open to a page) becomes the ghost's tools for the night.
3. **Night:** the player, as the ghost, leaves a limited number of traces: circling words, cutting out words, or writing, depending on the tools available.
4. **Morning:** she finds the traces, decides whether to believe them, and can do a little more.

**Visual and sound direction (exploring).** Hand-drawn, pencil and light watercolor; a palette of black, white and grey with a damp green tint. Sound carries the humidity: dripping, fans, rain on corrugated roofs, the market below. **[TODO: 视觉风格还没定，这里按你给 GPT 的 prompt 写的]**

**Explorations so far and what I learned.**
- **Listing memories and mapping them** (notebook pp. 71–78; digital and hand-drawn maps). I found the core contradiction, "wanting the absolute ⇄ fading into fog," and learned that my material is my own and doesn't need to be borrowed.
- **Breaking down the opening of *Disco Elysium*.** Seven principles, e.g. start after the lowest point, give each inner voice a recognizable sound, let the player choose whether to believe their self-myth. I also learned not to copy: my first scene attempt became the mirror scene with a different prop.
- **Analyzing *Silver Pines Town*.** How lighting, minimal UI and diegetic objects (a radio, a phone booth) carry atmosphere and story.
- **Class critique.** My first pitch, "a game *for* people who feel lost," sounded like a product serving users. I learned to describe what the game is *about*, and that resonance comes from specific details.
- **Premise development** (notebook pp. 60–61, 95–96). From "a person who forgets everything each morning" to a ghost who can only use what she left out, to a protagonist who forgets her dreams rather than her whole self.
- **Character design.** A first sketch of the protagonist: shoulder-length thin hair, dark circles, many empty piercing holes, an expression that always looks lost while she is in fact highly socialized and has learned to say what people want to hear.

## Personal Evaluation Rubric

**The piece**
- **Complete and playable:** a finished 30–60 minute game with a beginning, middle and end, not only a demo. *Measure:* a first-time player can finish it without my help.
- **The rules read without explanation:** *Measure:* in playtests, players understand the day/night loop and the "you can only use what she left out" rule within the first five minutes.
- **The choice has weight:** *Measure:* in paper and digital playtests, players hesitate over what to leave each night and can explain why they chose it.
- **Specific, not private:** *Measure:* playtesters from outside my background can retell the story and name something they recognized; friends from China say it feels true to the place.

**The process**
- Write and revise regularly instead of waiting for the perfect idea. *Measure:* the opening scene is rewritten at least three times; a biweekly blog post documents progress.
- Test on paper before building. *Measure:* at least one paper prototype session before starting the Unity build.
- Learn the tools: Unity and Yarn Spinner. *Measure:* a playable "Day 1" vertical slice by the end of January.

**For me**
- Make something that comes from what I actually care about, not what I think I should make. *Measure:* at the end, I can say what the game is about in my own words, and what my own preference is among its open answers.
