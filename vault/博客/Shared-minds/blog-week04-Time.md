Sometimes we think we’re in charge of our own thoughts, but most of our thinking actually happens passively. You can really see this when meditating. If we don’t actively focus and hold our minds steady, our thoughts wander easily. One idea pops up right after another, almost like our conscious mind isn’t calling the shots. If we can’t fully pick what thought comes next, could other people — or stories, algorithms and media — pick it for us? Storytellers, filmmakers and social media platforms all build controlled chains of ideas, images and feelings. They replace our natural, messy stream of thoughts with a carefully arranged path. This is exactly what Walter Murch talks about in _In the Blink of an Eye_, and what the Kuleshov Effect shows us visually: order creates meaning, even when there’s no real connection between things.

The Kuleshov Effect shows our brains automatically make cause-and-effect relationships just from the order of events. When a filmmaker cuts shots one after another, viewers don’t just see separate images. We feel logic, emotion and a story. That explains why we like having a storyteller guide our thinking. Our natural flow of thoughts is chaotic and full of conflicting possibilities. Stories simplify all that mess by giving us one clear line of thought. They save us the mental work of dealing with uncertainty and give us the comfort of direction, consistency and purpose. In that way, storytelling is a gentle kind of mental control. It does the thinking for us, so we don’t have to work through everything alone.

This works the same way as algorithms on apps like TikTok. Algorithms notice our brains crave constant, sequential stimulation and keep feeding us the “next thought” nonstop. Every video shapes what we see, feel and think next. It shrinks our wide range of possible thoughts down into one thin, steady slice of reality. Do these stories and algorithmic sequences make media a little “evil”? Not evil in the way of wanting to hurt people, but deceptive on a cognitive level. Every story hides how complicated things really are. It trades the endless, unclear, multi-sided nature of real thoughts and real life for a simple, straight-line plot. Stories tempt us to accept only one reading, one timeline, one version of “truth,” while wiping out the bigger, messier picture of life. This is especially true for people who spend hours scrolling short videos and algorithm-fed content. Over time, we get used to this easy, nonstop stream of mental stimulation. We slowly lose the drive to slow down and process information deeply. Long texts and complex videos that need real mental effort start to feel tiring and frustrating.

Permanently saved media makes this problem worse. When we write, post or lock in a thought online, we take away its natural fleeting quality. Those random passing thoughts — silly, annoying, biased or impulsive ones — get frozen forever. We can revisit and search them anytime. This lasting media has good sides: clearer expression, preserved memories, and the chance to strengthen valuable ideas and knowledge. Media lets important thoughts come back to us again and again, get refined, and grow more powerful in our minds. But the downsides are serious. Negative thoughts, quick judgments and temporary moods become permanent. Natural thoughts fade fast and correct themselves, but recorded ideas keep looping inside our heads. A lot of the time, it’s healthier for our minds when thoughts just pass away. Many thoughts deserve to disappear; they are unexamined, untrue or unkind. Only useful ideas should stay, and mental noise should fade naturally.

This also shows a big difference between a sequence and real time. Time itself is constant change, shapeless and open. But a thought sequence is man-made, broken into separate pieces and arranged in order. Our brains mistake ordered thoughts for real cause and effect, even when two back-to-back ideas have nothing to do with each other. As Murch says, film editing copies and hijacks how we feel time. It tricks us into sensing a logical progression when there is nothing but cuts and ordered shots. Our minds want connections between things, so we invent them.

Finally, thinking works in a recursive way. When a second thought follows the first, the first thought no longer stays the same. Whatever comes next reinterprets, reframes and changes the earlier thought after the fact. That means our old thoughts keep getting rewritten by whatever we think later. Stories, media and algorithms all take advantage of this recursion. By controlling which thought comes next, they control how we look back and understand everything that came before. In the end, all media doesn’t just record our thoughts. It reshapes the very way we experience time, ourselves and reality.

This week, I made a comic space where thoughts can unfold one frame at a time.

The user begins by entering their name and choosing a comic layout style. Every time they enter a new thought, Replicate generates an image and places it into the next comic panel. The user can choose between a dynamic Japanese manga style, a retro comic grid, or an American comic style. Each panel can also be dragged around, so the user can change the relationship between the images, and the layout of the panels may also shows differences of how people think.

One problem I wanted to address was the lack of continuity between AI-generated images. At first if I want to put a girl in different panel, the girl in every panel looks completely different, which is really annoying. So I added a continuity notes field where the user can describe recurring characters, clothing, locations, or visual details. When a new frame is generated, the prompt also includes the previous frames and these notes. This encourages the model to keep the same story world instead of inventing a completely different character every time.

The user can also decide how the comic should be read. The original thought text can be shown inside the panel or hidden. The comic frame and the generated image have separate controls, so the user can resize the frame without changing the image scale, or zoom the image without changing the frame.

I used Firebase Anonymous Authentication and Firestore to save the comic data. The user does not need to create a complicated account. Firebase creates an anonymous identity in the background and stores the user’s name, comic style, panel order, text, image URLs, and panel positions. When the user returns, they can search for their previous comics by name instead of having to remember an ID.

This project made me think about who decides what comes next in a comic. Is it the user, who enters the next thought, or is it the AI, which predicts the next visual moment based on everything that came before? Once the images are saved, rearranged, and played back as a sequence, they become more than isolated generations. They become a visual record of a thought process.
