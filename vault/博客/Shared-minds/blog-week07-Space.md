Foer started as a journalist covering the U.S. Memory Championship and, a year later, won it himself. His point is that memory champions don’t have extraordinary brains. They use an ancient technique, the memory palace (method of loci): you place what you want to remember inside a familiar space, like your childhood home, and attach vivid, absurd images to each item. (which remind me of the memory palace of Sherlock Holmes, Benedict Cumberbatch's version) What struck me most is that **our brains are bad at abstract information but remarkably good at space and imagery.** Mnemonic techniques are acts of translation, converting what we’re bad at into what we’re built for. That made me think of interface design as the same kind of translator. Foer also notes that we’ve outsourced memory to phones and notes apps. It’s convenient, but something is lost. It made me reflect on my own note-taking system: I’ve stored so much, but how much of it actually _lives_ in my head?

## My Writing

Space does help me think, but not by letting me _see_. It helps by letting me _locate_. When organizing my thesis research I used a keyword map. The concepts have no physical distance between them, yet once I placed them on a canvas, I started remembering “that idea is in the lower left, near _blindness_.” Position became a hook for retrieval, which is exactly Foer’s memory palace.

But because I study accessibility, I keep noticing that when we say “space,” we usually mean **visual space**. Blind people have a strong sense of space too, built from sound, touch, movement, and time. That makes me think space isn’t “the three dimensions in front of my eyes.” It’s a relationship: knowing where something is and how to get back to it. Seen that way, high-dimensional mathematical space and bodily space aren’t contradictory. Both are about nearness and paths.

On cinema versus VR, I prefer cinema’s cut. Thinking isn’t a continuous walk. It’s a jump, where one association takes you somewhere far away. I relate to the idea of “navigation by creation”: instead of walking through a world that already exists, each thought conjures a new space.

As for 8 billion parallel universes, embedding space offers one possibility. If everyone’s thoughts lived in the same space, then around any one of my ideas there might be strangers with similar ones. A location in space becomes a place to meet other people.

### The Memory Crypt

The Memory Crypt is a small 3D memory palace in the browser. You type the things you need to remember, an AI sculpts each one out of stone and wax, and it leaves them in the niches of a candlelit crypt. Then the candles go out and you have to remember what was where.

### Where it came from

The project grew out of the TED talk, Joshua Foer explains that memory champions don't have special brains. They use the method of loci: put each thing in a place you know, and make the image absurd. Our brains are bad at lists and very good at places and pictures, so the trick is translation.

So I treated the memory palace as an interface. The "nematode" part of me gets a room it can turn around in, and the symbolic part gets to decide what goes inside.

### The Story

You are a forgettable cellar keeper standing in the center of a circular stone chamber. There are 5–7 numbered niches on the surrounding walls, each lit by a candle.

You type in a list of things to remember.

The LLM turns each item into an absurd image and assembles it using basic three.js shapes

Each object is placed inside a niche, with a handwritten description beside it. You turn your view around with the mouse and memorize them.

Click "Lights Out", and the candles go out one by one, making the objects disappear.

Recall phase: The candles light up again one after another, but the niches are empty. You need to type what was originally there. If you answer correctly, the object comes back to life.

### Building it

The whole piece is one HTML file using three.js, with no build step. Three parts matter most.

**The look.** For the visual style I took cues from low-poly indie horror games: tiny resolution, a handful of colors, heavy dithering. The scene renders at 230 pixels tall into an off-screen texture. A shader then maps every pixel to one of three color ramps (cool teal, warm crimson, candle gold) and uses an ordered Bayer pattern to add the dither. Because of that, the models can be very rough. The shader and the candlelight do most of the work.

**The AI sculptor.** Following the week's prompt about letting a model build the world, each item goes to an LLM through ITP Replicate proxy. The model doesn't return an image. It returns a JSON list of primitives: sphere, box, cylinder, cone, torus, capsule, each with a color, position, scale and rotation. The page assembles them, scales the result to fit the pedestal and gives it a slow idle sway. All items are sent in parallel, so they appear one after another instead of all at the end.
