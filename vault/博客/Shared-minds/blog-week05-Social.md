### Designing Social Media for Common Ground

The first thing I notice when examining my mind is that I am alone with access to it. Other people cannot directly enter my thoughts. They can only encounter what I express through speech, writing, gestures, images, or other forms of media. At the same time, much of my thinking is shaped by other people. I think through language, and language is something I inherit from a community. My opinions, memories, ambitions, fears, and even my sense of identity are connected to conversations with others.

This creates a paradox: thought is private, but thinking is deeply social. We use media not only to communicate but also to think together. In this sense, almost every form of media can be considered social media. A diary may be private, but it is still written in a language created by society. A book is written by one person, but it enters a public conversation. A computer is not automatically social, yet networks turn computers into environments where people can cooperate, argue, learn, and form communities.

However, computers and networks may also weaken our ability to communicate well. Many online platforms reward speed, certainty, anger, and popularity. They encourage people to perform opinions rather than explore ideas. Short posts remove context, algorithms divide people into separate information environments, and constant phone use can replace direct social interaction. We may be more connected technically while becoming less comfortable with silence, disagreement, and face-to-face conversation.

Harari argues that shared stories allow large groups of people to cooperate. Nations, religions, companies, laws, and money all depend partly on stories that many people accept together. The challenge today is that everyone can distribute their own stories, while shared narratives are becoming harder to maintain. Culture wars fragment society because people are not only disagreeing about policies; they are living inside different interpretations of reality.

I do not think the solution is to force everyone to accept one story. A single dominant story can create unity, but it can also silence minorities and hide injustice. Averaging all stories is not enough either, because some experiences would disappear in the middle. A better model might be a network of many stories with visible connections between them. People should be able to maintain their own perspectives while also seeing where their experiences overlap with others.
