This chapter interrogates what thoughts actually are, questioning how science studies consciousness while overlooking the messy, lived texture of inner experience.

### Quotes & Reflections

> “Consciousness is not just another object of knowledge, but also, and more fundamental, that by which any object is knowable.”

We try to observe our own minds, yet the very tool we use for observation is consciousness itself. There is no fully “outside”, objective viewpoint to watch our thinking. This explains why simple introspection or meditation can feel tricky: when I notice my own thoughts, the act of noticing already changes what I am observing. It resonates with my meditation exercise for this assignment — trying to merely watch my mind‑stream without getting pulled into it is constantly disrupted by the act of watching.

> “No state once gone can recur and be identical with what it was before, because we are not the same.”

William James frames thought as a flowing river rather than a train of separate ideas. Every thought carries the aura, afterglow and context of everything that came before. This makes me rethink my sense of self. If my inner thoughts never repeat exactly, and every experience reshapes me, in what sense am I “the same person over time”? It is one of the questions from the assignment that I cannot give a neat answer to.

> “Inner speech… may actually not be all that common. […] Fewer than a quarter of the samples that Hurlburt has gathered report experiences of inner speech.”

I long assumed my thinking was mostly inner voice‑based self‑talk. Hurlburt’s DES sampling challenges that assumption. Many of my own thoughts seem to exist as unsymbolized thought: a sense of knowing something without words or clear mental images. This makes me wonder how much we miscommunicate when we assume everyone thinks with inner monologue.

> “Spontaneous thought represents a precious space of mental freedom and self‑creation, a space we should work to defend and expand. […] scrolling on smartphones has cut into the time we once spent in mind‑wandering and other forms of inner experience.”

Kalina Christoff‑Hadjilieva points out how modern daily life erodes unstructured mind‑wandering. Capitalist‑inflected values praise productive, goal‑oriented thinking and dismiss idle wandering as wasteful. Yet spontaneous wandering is where creativity and self‑understanding grow. I notice how my phone constantly interrupts my stream of consciousness, leaving less room for unforced, unproductive thought.

Neither science nor language can fully capture the full richness of inner experience. Literature, meditation, and even building creative web tools become alternative ways to engage with the stream of thought that resists clean description.

## Writing

> **Ask**
>
> How do you think? What is your stream of consciousness like? Do you feel like you are aware and in control of your thoughts? Do you decide to think your thoughts or do they just occur to you. Are you generally aware that you are thinking or are you usually "lost in thought?" Are your thoughts delivered as voices. Do your thoughts repeat? Are your thoughts mostly positive or negative? Do you think one thought at a time? Are you the same person over time? What makes one thought follow another? Do you always feel like there are many candidates for a connecting to a following thought? How can you better get to know how you think?

Looking back at my own thinking process, I find it depends heavily on my mental state at any given moment. When I actively practice meditation — my mom often meditates and does seated contemplation, and she has guided me through these exercises because I struggle with falling asleep — I notice that trying to force‑direct my thoughts, such as forcing my mind to go blank or following guided prompts and singing‑bowl background audio to “calm myself down”, actually makes my mind jump to random places more easily. For instance, during meditation when I try to relax fully and tune into bodily sensations, my attention drifts toward sounds. Noises around me readily trigger associations. If people nearby are talking, their words replay inside my head, appearing almost like movie subtitles. Sometimes I consciously pull my wandering associations back. Other times I drift completely away, until I suddenly remember I am supposed to focus on breathing or physical sensation — yet by then I have already forgotten what my previous train of thought was.

This experience reminds me of an observation from our reading: _“trying to catch a thought mid‑flight, it ceases forthwith to be itself. Catching a thought is as futile as trying to hold a snowflake.”_ When I realize my mind has wandered in meditation and attempt to recall the thoughts that just drifted by, those associations are already gone. The act of observing the stream of consciousness disturbs the very thing I want to observe.

During more unprompted mind‑wandering episodes — whether I zone‑out while sitting or spontaneous thoughts bubble up as I walk — my awareness of my own thoughts feels sharper. In these moments I do not deliberately control whether or what I think. It is somewhat like listening to an inner voice speaking inside my head. Still, inner‑speech is not the entirety of my thinking. Many associations exist only as a vague sense‑of‑knowing, without sound or distinct mental imagery. This corresponds to what the text calls **unsymbolized thought**: complete thoughts that can exist without inner speech or clear mental pictures.

Rather than a linearly‑flowing river, my consciousness feels more like this: one thought arises, then multiple branches extend outward from that topic. Eventually one of these branches grabs my attention. Sometimes I cannot tell whether I intentionally steer my associations in that direction, or whether some part of my inner‑self simply grows interested and keeps expanding along that thread. Once drawn in, new branches sprout from that selected idea again.

I realize here that **I do not think only one thought at a time**. At any given mental moment, several potential follow‑up lines of thinking hover in my mind. Most of these candidate branches never get fully explored. Only one captures my focus; the rest fade out of awareness and disappear entirely. This aligns with William James’ argument that thoughts are not discrete train‑like units. Every thought is surrounded by vague, unresolved associative fringes and halos.

Throughout this process I can perceive my thoughts and hold a degree of agency over them. For example, if my mind drifts toward negative or anxiety‑provoking ideas, I can consciously pull myself back or force my mind onto something different. Even so, most fragmented thoughts and this branching, brainstorm‑like mental activity do not feel like choices I actively decide upon. Instead thoughts well‑up spontaneously. As Christoff writes about **spontaneous thought**: many ideas emerge from within the mind and do not originate purely from external sensory input. Though I possess the ability to redirect my attention, the initial arrival of these thoughts is not fully under my command.

External sounds, fragments of memory, and present‑moment emotions all act as triggers that move one thought into the next. This answers what drives transitions between my ideas. My thoughts also repeat. Beyond replaying other people’s speech during meditation, anxious or worrisome fragments cycle into my awareness. I experience both light, free‑roaming reveries and stressful ruminations; positive and negative thoughts alike appear.

My mode of thinking shifts drastically across contexts: my stream of consciousness looks completely different in meditation, while wandering on foot, and while lying awake in bed with insomnia. This leaves me wondering: given how mutable my patterns of thought are, in what sense am I still the same person across time?

Truly coming to understand how I think carries inherent obstacles, because the act of observation itself disturbs thought. Meditation offers one path for inward noticing, yet it has its limits. Learning to use meditation to observe my thinking rather than forcefully regulate it requires considerable practice.
