## My Writing

I watched Bret Victor's *Inventing on Principle*. He runs a little platformer while editing its code, drags time backwards, and the character's jump turns into a trail of ghosts you can drag around. The second half of the talk moved me most. He says the demos are not the point. The point is that he has a principle: *creators need an immediate connection to what they create*. When he sees that principle being violated, he treats it as a wrong that needs to be put right.

When I started my thesis over this semester, I wrote that I had suddenly realized many of the things I made before were things I felt I *should* make. In the end, they were things I didn't hate but couldn't say I liked: things that were "right for the moment" or "might be useful," but not things I had a connection with.

### Level: where I put myself

I see myself as a designer who wants to use code, and every technical tool I know, to make the images in my head and my "vague feelings" real, and vibe coding makes that much easier. But if AI saves me the time I used to spend debugging, and I don't spend that time on something harder, I will just make more things I don't hate and don't like, only faster. By something harder I mean sharpening how I express myself, trying to catch or describe that "vague feeling" in my head and slowly getting closer to it, or, more importantly, finding what I really want to say, the things I have a connection with.

So the level I want to own is the level where **a feeling becomes a rule**. In a game, that level is the mechanic. In my thesis, *What She Left Out*, the main character is a math tutor in her twenties. She once wanted to study philosophy and write novels, but when the game begins, she no longer remembers ever having those thoughts. The player controls her, walking around an urban village, talking to people and following clues that open up new places. One of the puzzles is piecing words together: the player circles or cuts out words from newspapers, ads, things her family and neighbours say, and the drafts she once wrote, and pieces them into sentences to recover what she has forgotten. But some of these words come from her past self and some come from her family and the world around her, and there's no telling them apart. Even her old dream may have been shaped by a book or a teacher. That restriction *is* the feeling: not being able to tell which thoughts belong to you, and still having to answer "what do I want?"

### What I believe (for now)

**1. Make what I actually like, not what I should.**
But before that, what matters more is finding what I actually like, or allowing more possibilities while I'm looking, instead of arguing against every answer whenever I ask myself what I like.

**2. Restriction is a form of expression.**
Social media gives you endless actions, and every one of them is shallow: like, share, scroll, swipe. The things that have really moved me usually give me very few verbs, but each one carries weight. Fewer verbs, heavier choices.

**3. Let a feeling finish.**
To me, wholesome media lets you stay with something long enough for it to end on its own. A feed interrupts you every fifteen seconds and promises the next thing will be better. Silence, waiting and static can all be part of the content.

**4. Don't flatten people.**
Last week I wrote about the numbered universes in *Spider-Verse*: numbering makes a huge space navigable, but it also files people away. Embeddings are the same; a vector can be a filing cabinet. I want to use neural networks to find the *in-between*, not to sort people into boxes.

**5. The mind is a crowd.**
"There is nothing either good or bad, but thinking makes it so." And we don't think the way we think we think. What I love about *Disco Elysium* is that your skills are voices in your head that argue with you, flatter you and lie to you. That feels closer to my inner life than any app I've used. Neural networks are good at speaking in many voices. I'd rather use that to let the different parts of a person argue out loud than to tell them who they are.

**6. Forgetting can be a medium.**
Film took a while to discover that a cut feels natural to the mind. I think computational media may discover something similar about *forgetting*. A neural network doesn't store things; it keeps the gist and loses the details, and every time it recalls something, it comes back a little different. That is how human memory works. My thesis is about this too: what she forgets is not her whole self, but what she once wanted. When the pressure is at its worst, she even seals away what has been recovered with her own hands, blacking out the notebook and pushing the box back under the bed. A medium that remembers instead of stores, that drifts and lets things fade, may be a liberty only computation can take.

**7. Give failure dignity.**
The works I love take broken people seriously.

**8. Stay close to what I'm making.**
This one I borrow from Victor. Paper prototypes, playtests, small sketches: I want to see what a choice does right away, not plan it in my head for months.

## My Making: Borrowed Sentences

Colab: https://colab.research.google.com/drive/1LbKO6UYpaTd-_OK_tf-rgKly884hSfKU

This week I chose interpolation. But instead of interpolating between images, I interpolated between two sentences: given sentences A and B, which sentences do you pass on the way from A to B?

### How I did it

1. **Turn sentences into numbers.** I used a small multilingual model from Hugging Face (paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2), which turns each sentence into 384 numbers. The closer two sentences are in meaning, the closer their numbers.
https://huggingface.co/sentence-transformers/paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2
![[Pasted image 20261118034736.png]]
2. **Build a sentence pool.** I wrote 36 sentences from three sources: her old manuscripts, things her mother says, and the newspaper and neighbours. The three sources come from my thesis, where the main character can't tell which words are her own and which are other people's.
3. **Interpolate.** A is "I want to write a novel by the river." B is "Find a stable job so your mother can stop worrying." I blend the vectors of A and B in proportion, moving from all A to all B, and at each step I look for the closest sentence in the pool. A and B themselves don't count, and a sentence can't be used twice.
What the blending gives me is a list of numbers, not a sentence. No sentence sits exactly at that point, and the model can't turn the numbers back into words, so I can only borrow the closest sentence from the pool. To see how good each borrowing is, I also calculate two numbers at every step: where the borrowed sentence actually lands on the line from A to B (landed, where 0 is A and 1 is B), and how close it is to the blended point (fit, where 1 is a perfect match).
4. **Flip them over.** First you only see the chain of sentences. Only at the end does it show whose words each one was.
5. **Turn them into paper slips.** The chain becomes a row of paper slips pasted from A to B. Next to each slip is a scale from A to B: the ring is where I asked to look, the dot is where the borrowed sentence actually sits, and the red line between them is the cost of borrowing. The worse the fit, the fainter the ink on the slip. Hover over one and it flips over to show its source on the back.

This needs me to get hold of the vectors myself, blend them myself, and compare the blended vectors against the pool. Calling an API like Replicate can't do that, so I had to go down a level.

### What I noticed

- **Every sentence in between is borrowed, and none of them quite fit.** The model didn't write a single new sentence; at each step it could only pick the closest one from sentences that already existed, and the fit is only around 0.3 every time. No sentence actually sits at the blended point. That's the same idea as my game: the in-between state can only be pieced together from other people's words, and the pieces never fit quite right.
- **The middle is empty.** The borrowed sentences fall into two clumps. One sits around 0.4, on the writing side, where her own words and her mother's take turns. The other sits around 0.65: the civil service exam, a steady income, "Throw out that box of old things." Nothing lands between 0.45 and 0.6. At t = 0.5 it was looking right at the middle, but it still borrowed "When I write, I feel real.", which lands at 0.40. In her words, there is no sentence between wanting to write and needing to be stable; you can only jump from one side to the other. I didn't arrange this; it came out of the numbers.
- **Change the language and the path changes.** I first used a Chinese sentence pool. With the same A and B and the same method, it borrowed completely different sentences: in the Chinese version, the first sentence after leaving "write a novel" was her mother's "Can writing put food on the table?", and right in the middle was a student's question, "Teacher, did you always want to be a teacher?" The same meaning in a different language, and the "middle" is somewhere else.
