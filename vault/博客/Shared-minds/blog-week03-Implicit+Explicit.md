### Left
When collaborating with another person, I would prefer to receive a Google Doc over a screenshot, an Illustrator file over a JPEG, and a MIDI file over an MP3. Because screenshots, JPEGs and MP3s are flattened, they just capture what something looks or sounds like in one fixed final state, but I cannot easily edit individual components inside them. By contrast, Google Docs, Illustrator files and MIDI store **addressable, rearrangeable discrete parts**. A collaborator can change one note in MIDI, move one vector shape in Illustrator, or rewrite one paragraph in Google Doc without destroying the whole work. This opens genuine dialogue: partners can propose partial changes, test alternatives, and build ideas together.

Breaking content into separate, addressable, rearrangeable building‑blocks is the core logic behind Pinker’s **discrete combinatorial system** and Deutsch’s **jump to universality**. Instead of blending elements together so their identities disappear (like mixing paint), each unit keeps its own meaning and can be reordered, swapped, removed or recombined following simple rules. This mechanism unlocks a vast “space of expression”: from a limited small set of basic pieces, you can generate a huge, practically unlimited number of brand‑new unique outputs. Human language works exactly this way: finite words combine via syntax to produce endless new sentences, far more than anyone could memorize beforehand. DNA genetic code operates on this same principle, using four nucleotide building‑blocks to encode countless distinct living organisms. General‑purpose digital computers are another major example of this jump‑to‑universality: built from simple on‑off switches, they can simulate almost any physical process.

Critics may argue that building from fixed pre‑made parts feels too much like “paint‑by‑numbers”, as if creativity gets constrained by pre‑existing building blocks. It is true that you are limited to your available set of components. But this criticism misunderstands how these systems work. Discrete combinatorial systems do not force you into only a small number of pre‑set results. Their power lies in recombination: novel, unexpected outputs emerge from new arrangements, even when the basic parts stay unchanged.

This powerful combinatorial idea raises harder questions for visual media. Words and DNA naturally fit discrete combinatorial rules, but images are often treated as continuous blended wholes. Still, we can imagine a visual language built on addressable changeable parts. Picture a landscape scene. Its modifiable discrete components could include: sky colour, cloud shapes and position, lighting direction, tree type and placement, ground texture, animal figures, and foreground objects. We could design a simple visual syntax with rules: trees must sit on ground layers; light sources determine shadow direction; clouds float above terrain. Each element stays independent and can be swapped, repositioned or deleted without ruining the whole scene. This would be our invented visual combinatorial language.

We can extend this logic further to moving images and film. Traditional film is normally “flattened” into finished video frames, much like a JPEG image. But imagine an “un‑flattened” cinematic medium. Instead of storing only rendered pixel frames, it stores discrete reusable components: character models, props, lighting setups, background environments, camera movements, and audio tracks. Every part remains editable after initial creation. A collaborator could reposition a character, change the time of day for lighting, swap background scenery, or adjust camera angles, without re‑shooting everything from scratch. This resembles modern real‑time 3D engines, but taken further as a general creative medium. It would shift film from a fixed recorded statement toward something more like human language: open‑ended, recombinable, and open to ongoing conversation and revision.


### Right
A lot of what we know cannot be written down as clear facts or rules. We call this implicit knowledge. For example, understanding jokes, feeling empathy for another person, or reading a social situation are things we do naturally, but it is hard to write step‑by‑step instructions for them. This lines up with McGilchrist’s idea about two ways our brains understand the world.

The left‑brain “Emissary” mode works with explicit knowledge: it breaks things apart into separate, reusable pieces and follows clear rules. The right‑brain “Master” mode focuses on the big picture, context, and subtle human feelings. McGilchrist says this right‑brain holistic way of seeing things is under‑valued in modern life, even though it gives us many of life’s most meaningful experiences. I mostly agree with him. Empathy, humour and picking up on unspoken social cues really cannot be fully reduced to simple rules. Still, big‑picture intuition is not perfect either. It can be biased or wrong, so we still need logical, analytical thinking to check it. Neither way of thinking works well alone.

Traditional computers work really well with the left‑brain style that Pinker and Deutsch talk about. Old‑school code runs on clear “if‑then” rules and rearranges separate small parts. But computers struggle with the fuzzy, context‑dependent things that rely on right‑brain‑style holistic thinking. They cannot naturally understand sarcasm, subtle moods or complicated real‑life situations without lots of hand‑written rules.

This brings up McGilchrist’s concern: computers might make our cultural imbalance worse. Our society already loves taking things apart into modular, recombinable components. Traditional computing rewards this kind of thinking and pushes holistic, messy context‑based judgements to the side. We risk treating our simplified broken‑down “map” of reality as the real world itself. That said, computers are just tools. The harm comes from how people choose to use them, not from computing itself.

Modern machine learning does not only rely on hard‑coded “if statements”. It finds hidden patterns and connections inside huge piles of data. Can this get us closer to something like the right‑brain holistic way? Large language models learn relationships between words, but the real world is not made of words. LLMs can sound reasonable, but they do not truly understand real‑world physical experience. Diffusion models learn how pixels relate to one another and can generate very realistic images. Even so, they only work with pixel data, they do not truly “see” or understand a scene the way a human does. They make convincing blended‑looking outputs, but this is not genuine holistic understanding.

Even with those limits, machine‑learning gives us an interesting middle ground. AI can hunt for useful, recombinable features inside messy real‑world data, similar to how the left brain breaks things down. Then generative models can put those pieces back together into complete, believable wholes. In theory this lets us get benefits from both approaches: the power of discrete recombination and the feel of a complete, contextual whole.

Right now AI only finds statistical patterns. It can mimic results that look like human holistic thinking, but it has no real empathy, genuine insight or sense of meaning. Machine‑learning can make better tools for us, but it cannot fix our society’s imbalance between narrow analysis and big‑picture thinking.

After working with the first version, I felt that keeping the words in two fixed columns was too limiting. So I added free dragging, multi-selection, and rearrangement. In this project, position works in a similar way to grammar. A word placed above, below, beside, or overlapping another word can suggest a different action or relationship.

Each item on the canvas is stored as a JSON object. It contains the item’s text or image, source, position, rotation, layer, animation, and type. The canvas is controlled by one JavaScript array.
