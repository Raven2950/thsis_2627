## My Writing

I watched *Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse* and found a few things in it that interested me.

### 1. Numbering the universes

Many shots keep stressing "Earth-42." There is something light, even a little bureaucratic and funny, about numbering: an infinite multiverse casually labelled with a string of digits, like a library call number.

But it also reminds you that you are only one of many. Every Spider-Man believes they are the only one, and the moment a number appears, that uniqueness is taken away. In the second film, the Spider-Society turns this numbering and sorting into a whole system for managing people and deciding who is "normal" and who is an "anomaly." The number goes from a handy label to a form of control. So it has two sides: it makes a huge space navigable, but it also risks reducing people and filing them away.

I think the numbering also keeps echoing the idea of parallel universes. As far as we know, our world hasn't confirmed whether parallel universes exist; at least we can't observe them. Still, I sometimes imagine a version of me in a parallel world, living a different life. When I'm in pain, I sometimes picture a me in another universe who is suffering more, or who is happier, and it comforts me.

### 2. Comic panels and monologues

Whenever a Spider-Man from another universe enters, comic panels appear along with that Spider-Man's monologue: "Okay, let's do this one more time..."

Each Spider-Man tells their origin with almost the same rhythm: bitten, gaining powers, losing someone important, learning responsibility. The skeleton of the story repeats, but each one tells it in a completely different style: black-and-white noir, Japanese anime, a cartoon pig. Despite the similarities, each keeps the rules of their own universe instead of being flattened into one style, which I find really interesting.

This "variation within repetition" is key. Because the skeleton is the same, the audience instantly recognises "oh, this is another Spider-Man," and they immediately feel related; their real individuality lives in the places where they depart from the skeleton.

And the monologue is each character telling their own story. Each one chooses how to present their life, compressing the "previously on" into a few seconds with a handful of panels and a few lines of caption. The comic form lets time jump, compress and sit side by side, without having to play out continuously.

### 3. Glitch: not belonging here

Spider-People from other universes keep glitching in a universe that isn't theirs, which I also find interesting. The glitch gives me the feeling that someone from another universe can't keep their body stable in a world they don't belong to: they flicker and shift out of place in bursts, and it hurts. So the glitch is really a visualisation of "not belonging here."

What's interesting is that the glitch is caused by the meeting itself. If everyone stayed in their own universe, nothing would be out of place; it is precisely because people from different worlds come together that instability appears. The ending of the first film makes this clear: in the end everyone has to go back to their own universe. The connection is real, but they can't coexist forever. This gives "connection" in the film a cost and a tension, rather than simple warmth.

## My metaphor: a radio

I didn't want to make a Spider-Verse-style interface, so I looked for something that could hold these ideas without borrowing the film's imagery. In the end I thought of an old radio.

What I love about radio is that every station is always present in the air around you at the same time. Hundreds of voices are layered in the same space; you just can't hear them. When you turn the dial, you don't go anywhere. You pull one signal out of the superimposed ones and bring it into focus. This is very close to the idea of navigating by conjuring rather than travelling, and very like many realities superimposed on each other until your observation "collapses" them into one. (I really love the idea of "collapse," too!)

### Broadcast yourself

When you enter, you open a station of your own. You can talk, write text to be read aloud, or record the sound of where you are: rain, the subway, the dog downstairs. Late-night radio has a particular quality: speaking to strangers you can't see makes you more honest. After you leave, your station keeps playing. Someone might tune in to you at any time.

### Your frequency

You don't choose your frequency. What you say decides your place on the spectrum through embeddings, so people saying similar things end up close together. As you keep talking, your frequency slowly drifts, passing different neighbours.

A dial has only one dimension, and people don't. I'm considering a few approaches:

- **Bands** (like AM / FM / shortwave): each band is a different dimension, and when you switch bands, your neighbours change completely.
- **A spectrum that rearranges around you.** Every time you stop, the scale reorganises, so the people on either side are always the ones closest to you right now. The space is generated around you, not a fixed map.
- **An antenna.** The dial searches in one direction; turning the antenna changes the direction.

### Connection as interference

The part I'm most excited about is what happens between stations. When two frequencies are close, they bleed into each other. You're listening to your own station when someone else's voice suddenly cuts in, broken up and full of static. In this interface, that crosstalk is the match. The moment of connection is the moment someone's voice seeps into yours.

From there you can keep turning the dial, tune in precisely and enter their station, or deliberately stop in between and let the two voices coexist. One step further, you can "bleed through" and let your voice join their channel for a while, like Spider-People who reach another universe and sense each other through their "spider-sense."

I also want to use a physical phenomenon: when two close frequencies overlap, they produce a slow swelling and fading called "beats." The closer the two frequencies, the slower the beats, almost like breathing. This can express closeness through rhythm instead of a percentage.

### Static

Most of the time, what you hear will be static. I think that's fine. Static isn't empty; it's signal that is too weak or too mixed to make out. It can feel lonely, but it also feels like anticipation: there is something in there, just not close enough yet. Denser static might mean a crowd of faint voices.
