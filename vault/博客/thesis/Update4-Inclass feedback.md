In class I described my project in one sentence:

> It is a story-driven narrative game for people that feel lost between who they were expected to be and who they actually are, and have problems figuring out the meaning of life and want to make their life more controlled.

Then my classmates asked questions. The point of this round wasn't for me to answer them. It was to hear the things I hadn't thought about. These are their questions, and what I'm taking from them.

## What they asked
![[IMG_3323 1.jpg]]

- If I'm not your audience, could I still enjoy your game? Will my life be better if I play it?
- What does making a choice mean in your game?
- So many people are lost in different ways, and everyone's experience is different. How can one game with one "universal lost person" speak to any audience? How do you define lost?
- What story will you use? What would "the meaning of life" be in the game? What idea do you want to convey?
- Are you telling a story *of* being lost, or are you trying to make people *feel* lost?
- You described people who fall short of expectations, people who aren't sure about the meaning of life, and people who don't feel in control of their lives. Those are three different, sometimes overlapping, experiences. Is there a priority among them?

## What I noticed about my own sentence

Many of the questions came from how I framed it. "A game **for** people who feel lost" sounds like a product that serves a group of users, so people naturally asked: who is the user, what problem does it solve, does it work for everyone, will it make my life better?

But that isn't what I want to make. I want to make an **expressive** work: an original story built from things that trouble me. I also have to admit that I don't yet know what I want players to feel after playing. I don't think I fully understand what Disco Elysium is trying to say either, but that doesn't stop me from loving it and feeling something. It reached me because it was **specific** — the hungover face in the mirror, the "superstar" option, the cold voices — not because it tried to speak for everyone.

So instead of describing who the game is *for*, I should describe what it is *about*.

## What I want to think about next

**1. Telling it, or making the player feel it.**
This is the biggest one. The player can watch a character be lost, or the player can be put into that state. I touched this in my Disco Elysium breakdown (the drunk walk, the body's state changing the controls), and I wrote that my own "brain fog, nearsighted, faded" might become changes in the visuals or controls. Which parts of my game should be told, and which should be felt?

**2. Choice as control.**
One of my themes is wanting control. In a game, making a choice *is* control. So choices don't have to be just a mechanic; they can be the theme itself. The game could give control and take it away. Choices could look meaningful and change nothing. Maybe the harder the player tries to control things, the less they can. (Disco Elysium's GDC talk is literally called *Meaningless Choices and Impractical Advice*.)

**3. Three experiences, or one chain?**
My classmates split "falling short of expectations," "not knowing the meaning of life," and "wanting control" into three things and asked which one comes first. Maybe they're a chain: growing up measured against other people's expectations → fear of failing, wanting one absolute, controllable answer → not finding it, and everything fogging over and losing meaning. If so, that chain might be the structure of the story. I want to check it against my memory map.

**4. Why would someone who isn't "the audience" keep playing?**
Recognition alone isn't enough. Disco Elysium also holds people with absurdity, humor, mystery and its writing. In my last update I wrote that my material is quieter. What keeps players going through something quiet?

**5. The word "lost."**
When people kept asking me to define "lost," maybe it was the wrong word. My own material almost never says "lost." It says fog, fading, nearsighted, verdicts. My own words are probably more precise.

The question "will my life be better?" matters less to me right now. The part worth keeping is whether the ending offers any hope, and that can wait until the story exists.

## How I changed my description

After class I rewrote my one-line description for the alumni chats form, from "for people who feel lost" to what the game is about:

> I'm working on a story-driven narrative game (likely in Unity with Yarn Spinner, inspired by Disco Elysium) about an original character who grew up measured against other people's expectations and searches for one absolute answer in a life that feels foggy, drawing on things I struggle with myself.

I also turned the questions above into what I want to ask alumni: whether to tell a foggy, out-of-control story or make players feel it, what choices mean in a game about wanting control, how to turn personal questions into fiction without it being too private, and how to scope a complete game by May.
