## Part Three: Writing

### The team and the process

- **Eight credited writers, and blurry lines between them.** 
- **New writers started with "lore lectures."** It took a new writer four to six months to write independently. The first month was mostly Robert explaining the world of Elysium, like going to university.
- **The tool changed how they wrote.** They wrote dialogue in Articy:Draft, short lines connected node by node. Helen says that if you stare at a blank Word document, nothing comes out; writing one dialogue box after another in Articy was so much fun that, for the first time, the team went from "writing too little" to "writing too much."
- **There is no "lore bible."** Robert says Elysium has no written setting bible. It's like jazz: one artist teaches another, and over years you learn how to "play" it. It isn't an IP. It's a way of thinking and dreaming, and everything has to work around one very specific emotion.
- **Good work comes from rewriting.** The process: a kick-off meeting with Robert about the character → a first draft → editing it together, dialogue card by dialogue card → rewriting if the direction is wrong. Helen says they weren't geniuses and the first drafts weren't good; the only thing they were good at was "editing, editing, editing." Reportedly 70% of the game is a rewrite, and half of that is a rewrite of a rewrite. Cuno was rewritten about 15 times, Evrart about 7. Argo says you should never be pressured to put in low-quality work just because it already exists.
- **The dialogue structure is actually quite traditional.**

### Characters

- **Two characters are easier to write than one.** While writing René and Gaston, Argo found that with two characters in a scene, they can play off each other, so you don't get stuck at "well, somebody should say something." He's best at writing men in their fifties with drinking problems, because before ZA/UM he worked manual jobs where most of his colleagues were like that.
- **In a serious moment, give the joke to someone else.** Helen's favorite is the Working Class Woman, who seems ordinary but has more and more story the more you talk to her; in her apartment you find clues that she has kids. The hardest part was the scene where you tell her that her husband is dead. Harry is such a funny character, so how do you make him understand this is no place for jokes? In the end, it's the woman herself who makes the jokes.
- **Someone who wants an explanation for everything.** Helen says the bookstore owner, Plaisance, protects herself with spirituality, because Revachol is so unstable. A small business owner always feels "I don't know what's going to happen," and just wants an explanation for everything that happens.
- **A voice is a rhythm.** Cuno looks easy to write: swear a lot and refer to yourself in the third person. But Argo says the hard part is finding the rhythm, like a weird, melodic rap; say "Cuno" too many times in a sentence and it gets annoying, so you tune it like an instrument. Now and then the mask has to slip, and he says "I," showing a crack of vulnerability. After weeks of editing Cuno, your brain starts thinking in his rhythm, and you have to "de-Cuno" yourself.
- **Let each writer write what they know.** Robert is good at placing people: he had Olga, who had taught college writing, write the academics, and told her to write Ruby as a Californian, because that's something she knows.
- **Lena was too hard to write:** she knows so much about the world that every draft turned into a lore dump, while the player has only just woken up. Almost every writer wrote a version of Lena.

### Harry and Kim

- **Amnesia can just be a stupid joke.** Robert thought no one could do an amnesia story after *Planescape: Torment*, until he found a new angle: what if he just drank so much he can't remember? You wake up knowing nothing about yourself, except that you're wearing giant '70s bootcut pants. Every object you find is a piece of bad news about yourself, and the final blow is that you're a cop, and you don't feel like one at all.
- **Kim started as a function and became a feeling.** Kim was originally designed to remind the player where they are in the main quest, to balance a player who wanders around and picks the most outrageous options. So at first he was very dry. As everyone kept writing, he started offering compassion when the player messes up.
- **A "dream friend simulator."** Robert played the game once before launch and only then realized that Kim had made it a "dream friend simulator." His earlier novel was "hostile": a mystery with no answer, concerned only with its own language. The game was a reaction to the novel. It's devoted to serving the player, and Kim is the part that holds your hand.
- **Everyone wrote Harry and Kim.** Every writer had to write Harry's and Kim's lines in their own characters' dialogues. Later Olga suggested that Justin edit all of Kim's lines, and that's when Kim became consistent.

### Mysteries and the Pale

- **The church was pieced together by everyone, and it was chaotic.** It started with a concept painting of a church on stilts, and then people added to it one by one: the Pale, the speed freaks in the tent, the Crab Man, Noid, Soona. Robert says this isn't the right way to do things; it felt more like constantly keeping it from becoming a catastrophe. Writers often secretly finished other people's abandoned work, and the tension kept building.
- **The Pale stays in the background.** They didn't want the whole game to be about chasing the Pale. People go on living; it's just a fact of this world.
- **Never give the answer.** Why did Harry lose his memory? Too much alcohol, getting too close to the Pale, denial, or is he faking it? The writers never agreed among themselves, and they left plausible evidence for every theory. Robert says nothing truly interesting can have a concrete answer, only theories; as Alan Moore says in *From Hell*, Jack the Ripper will always be a superposition of all our theories.
- **But the writer needs a preference.** Robert says the storyteller has to have their own leaning, but can never say it out loud. His example is David Lynch, who once gave out "nine clues" for watching *Mulholland Drive*. They don't help you solve it at all, but they tell you he isn't just throwing dreamy things at you; there's a logic underneath. Being both open and systematic is how you have it both ways.
- **Invent new mysteries.** Robert thinks *Lost* was exciting at first because people felt there was a completely new trope underneath it; once they realized it was an existing trope, the excitement was gone. So the Pale can't be any existing trope. It isn't global warming, and it isn't a metaphor. Players don't know this world's rules, so they can't guess the answer.

### Robert's favorite characters

- **His own state went into a character.** Near the end, the investors were angry that the ending wasn't finished, and Robert was very sad. The burnt-out, dead feeling of the Deserter on the island was exactly how he felt at the time.
- **Dolores Dei and the Phasmid.** One is sacred and huge, yet appears in the guise of Harry's ex, the epitome of the "new type of mystery." With the other, he could still write something gentle and childlike after so much darkness. In the Phasmid scene there's a choice: would you rather be an animal like it, or are you glad to be yourself? Robert says most players pick the second, but the first might be more healing for Harry.
- **Research helps you find the tone.** His research assistant researched autopsies, and also stick insects. Once he wrote down the strange, childlike little story of the Phasmid's broken antenna growing back as a foot, the tone of the whole dialogue was set: creepy, and innocent at the same time.

## My reflection

**1. Find a tool that keeps me writing.**
Articy took them from "can't write" to "writing too much," because dialogue boxes are short, one after another, with no blank page to face. On my "Me" page I wrote that I can't express what I feel. Maybe I shouldn't write the story in a document at all, but write dialogue directly in a node-based tool like Yarn Spinner, one line and one choice at a time.

**2. The first draft doesn't need to be good.**
Cuno was rewritten 15 times, and 70% of the game is a rewrite. This matters to me, because I'm always interrogating myself, writing an answer and crossing it out, 100% or nothing. Disco Elysium's writers didn't get it right the first time; they just kept editing. So for the one-page scene in Exploration 7, the goal should be a first draft I can edit, not getting it right.

**3. No rulebook, but one emotion.**
Robert says Elysium has no setting bible, only one very specific emotion that everything works around. I still don't know what my story is, but maybe what I need to find first isn't the setting but that emotion. My map already has a candidate: "wanting the absolute ⇄ fading into fog."

**4. Two voices are easier to write than one.**
Argo says that with two people in a scene, they can play off each other. Disco Elysium's inner voices work the same way: one voice finishes, and another pushes back. My inner voices (Mom, the fallen prodigy, the interrogator) have always shown up alone and passed judgment alone. If they talked over each other and argued in one scene, it might be more dramatic than a cold monologue.

**5. Don't give the answer, but have one.**
This is the point most directly connected to my theme. My core contradiction is "wanting one absolute answer," and Disco Elysium's writing refuses to give one: there are several theories about why Harry lost his memory, and even the writers never settled on one. But Robert also says the storyteller needs a preference, and players need to feel there's a logic underneath. Maybe my game could refuse to give an answer in its very form, so that someone who wants an answer never gets just one; but I need to know which one I lean toward.
In class I said, "I don't yet know what I want players to feel." After this episode, I think that isn't necessarily a weakness; Disco Elysium's writers didn't agree on many things either. But "not knowing" and "not saying" are different, and in the end I still need my own preference.

**6. A quiet story needs a Kim too.**
A classmate asked why someone who isn't the "audience" would play. One of Disco Elysium's answers is Kim. He turns a game about failure and collapse into a "dream friend simulator." Robert's novel had no Kim, so it was "hostile"; the game has Kim, so it serves the player. My material is quiet and cold, and most of the voices pass judgment. Maybe I need a character who doesn't judge, who offers a little compassion when the player messes up.

**7. Someone who wants an explanation for everything.**
Helen says Plaisance protects herself with spirituality because the world is so unstable that she wants an explanation for everything. That's very close to what I wrote about "wanting a controllable answer." In Disco Elysium, this need doesn't belong only to the hero; a bookstore owner has it too. Maybe my story doesn't have to put this need on the protagonist alone. It could be spread across different people, each looking for certainty in a different way.

**8. A state can be written into a character.**
The Deserter's burnt-out feeling was how Robert felt while finishing the game. I don't have to write my own experiences as they happened, but the state I was in can become the texture of a character. This also answers something I keep asking: how to use personal experience without making it too private.

## Questions: how do I start writing?

1. Disco Elysium is many small dialogues written in one world, connected non-linearly and split among different writers. How did it stay consistent? Even if I write the whole script myself, I easily write characters out of character (OOC).
2. Is writing a narrative game linear or fragmented?
3. How detailed does the main story need to be before I can start writing the other pieces?
4. I have no idea how big a game to make or where to start writing.

### 1. The pieces aren't linear. How do you keep them consistent?

Disco Elysium has no setting bible. It relies on four things:

- **One core emotion.** Every piece revolves around it. Before writing any scene, ask: is this scene one side of that emotion?
- **A fixed dialogue structure.** Every Disco Elysium dialogue goes "greeting → central hub → branching questions → back to the hub." With the same structure, however many pieces there are, they read like one game.
- **One person does a pass over each character.** At first everyone wrote Kim, each differently; he only became stable once Justin edited all of his lines.
- **Edit card by card.** Finishing a draft isn't the end. You go back and read it dialogue card by dialogue card.

**How I can avoid OOC writing on my own:**
- **Make a "voice card" for every character:** 
  - what they want and what they fear;
  - how they speak: sentence length, verbal tics, what they call the protagonist;
  - **things they would never say**;
  - three to five sample lines.
  **This is basically designing every character like an OC (original character), so maybe I should look at how other people design their OCs.**

- **Edit by character, not by scene.** When everything is written, pull out one character's lines from every scene and read them in a row, the way Justin edited Kim. OOC lines are invisible inside a single scene and obvious when read together.

### 2. Is narrative game writing linear or fragmented?

Both: **one linear spine, with pieces hanging off it.**

- **The spine**: Disco Elysium's spine is "solve a murder in a few days." It has clear beats the player will always pass through.
- **The pieces**: most NPCs and side quests are fairly independent units, like the Working Class Woman, Cuno, or the Phasmid. They aren't connected by plot order but by **state**: what the player knows, what they've done, how high their skills are, which unlocks or changes certain lines.

The key: **each piece has to stand on its own, and only echo other pieces optionally**. For example, "if the player has already met A, add a line here," not "you have to have met A to understand this." That way, whatever order the player goes in, nothing breaks.

### 3. How much of the main story do I need before writing the pieces?

You don't need to think up the whole world.
**What I must decide before I start:**

1. **The core emotion or question**, in one sentence.
2. **The protagonist**: what they want, what they actually need, and some basic background.
3. **The direction of the ending**: not a specific plot, but where the protagonist ends up. For example, "they don't find the answer, but no longer need it," or "they're still looking."
4. **3–5 beats on the spine**: the opening, a few turns, the ending.
5. **The world rules the pieces will touch**: decide only the parts that come up, and leave the rest blank.

**What can be decided while writing:** details of side characters, side quests, and most of the world.

### 4. How big? Where do I start?

**Scope (a rough first estimate, to adjust later):** I'm making it alone, with about six months until May, and I also have to build it in Unity and make the art. A realistic target:

- 30–60 minutes of play;
- one main location, like a building or a street, possibly with a few rooms;
- 3–5 characters, 2–4 inner voices;
- roughly 15,000–30,000 words of dialogue (a rough estimate; Disco Elysium has about a million).

Disco Elysium itself is a "vertical slice" of one corner of a much bigger plan. What I'm making is also one small, complete corner.

**Where to start (following Disco Elysium):**
1. **Write the opening first.** The first thing Disco Elysium had was the "waking up" demo, because the opening is the best test of whether the player plugs into the character right away.
2. **Make the opening playable.** Write one page, put it into Yarn Spinner, and play it myself. This one short piece, complete from start to finish, is my "vertical slice."
3. **Write the 3–5 beats of the spine, one sentence each.**
4. **Finally, hang pieces on the spine**, writing each one with voice cards.
