## Part One: Foundations
- **A group of dropouts and a tabletop game.** Robert Kurvitz dropped out of high school and played a Finnish knock-off of D&D with other dropouts. The system was thin, and because it wasn't good, they immediately wanted to make a better one. From about 15 to 22, that high-fantasy setting slowly became Elysium: first a tasteful steampunk with the elves and dwarves thrown out, then, with the steampunk washed out, something '70s-looking, with plastic phones and the feel of a cop show.
- **Three campaigns, and a world growing layer by layer.** The first, "World Autumn," was an amnesia story (inspired by *Planescape: Torment* and Alan Moore's *From Hell*). The second was like *Lord of the Flies*, with kids trapped in underground catacombs. The third followed two cop partners, inspired by the TV series *The Shield*, and was already close to what Disco Elysium became.
- **Old versions weren't thrown away. They became the world's history.** Even the original high-fantasy setting became the world's "Bronze Age." Robert got into Hegel, a philosopher of history, and used all that material to lay down thousands of years of history underneath the world.
- **"Putting the player into the shoes of the character."** Robert learned this from years as a game master: the opening has to hook you right away, and the first scene has to be personal, so the player plugs straight into the character. He says Harry waking up is the end result of all those tests.
- **Estonia, post-Soviet.** They grew up in post-Soviet Tallinn: punk bands, a bohemian scene straight out of Bulgakov's *The Master and Margarita*, harsh neoliberal reforms. Robert says the game doesn't speak for Estonia. It speaks for **being lost in history, having lost your planetary homeland** (the Soviet Union). Revachol is not Tallinn, and also a little bit is.
- **The novel failed, so he switched to games.** Robert wrote a novel set in this world, *Sacred and Terrible Air*. It took five years and sold about 1,500 copies; the rest were shredded. He still believed in the world and thought it might need another medium: games. With Rostov, he turned the cop campaign into a pitch for an isometric RPG, and eventually they got investment.

## Part Two: Building Elysium
- **The studio was in a half-derelict hippie commune.** Two old buildings were joined through broken walls, stairwells and a shared roof. In winter it was cold enough for gloves, water leaked into the server room, and once a gas heater nearly suffocated a colleague.
- **The building became the game's locations.** Its winding interior inspired the hidden paths through the Whirling-in-Rags. The building held a bookstore, a tattoo shop and a gallery, like a labyrinth, which became the game's "doomed commercial area." The punching bag became the gym there. With no whiteboard, they wrote on a furnace, and that furnace made it into the game too. One team member wondered what they would have made in a white, minimalist modern office.
- **"Us against the world."** No one in Estonia had made a game before, and everyone told them it wouldn't work. The crumbling building, the lack of money and a team that had never made a game made them unusually close. Helen calls that time an almost "mythical childhood."
- **Disco Elysium exists because the scope was cut.** The original plan was *The Return*, a huge cop RPG with combat. A programmer told Robert that two programmers and €700,000 could not build a combat system. Robert said fine, no combat, and a week later realized combat could be done with dice rolls and dialogue. Then he forced himself, in one night, to cut the story down to something people could start on the next day. The final game is really a "vertical slice" of one corner of Martinaise from the bigger plan.
- **Talking skills grew out of the writing.** In the first test dialogue, the Encyclopedia skill chimed in with trivia about a boxer, the hero asked whether there was any news on his own name, and it said no. Everyone found it hilarious. That is where skills became characters.
- **Writing is the gameplay.** With no combat, text was all the gameplay, so cutting text meant cutting the game. The final text came to about 600,000 words. The programmers were always patching the build for the next release date and never had time for a calm architecture.

## My reflection

**1. The world wasn't thought up at once. It grew in layers.**
Elysium took more than a decade, three campaigns and a failed novel to become Disco Elysium, and the old versions weren't deleted. They became the world's history. This makes me less anxious about my own progress: my memory list, my map and my breakdown notes may not mean "I haven't found the story yet." They may be the story's layers.

**2. An "immediate hook" is something you practice.**
In my breakdown of the opening I wrote about "starting from rock bottom." The documentary shows that Harry waking up wasn't a flash of inspiration. It came from Robert testing, campaign after campaign, how to plug a player into a character. So I should write my own opening several times and test it, instead of waiting for the perfect idea.

**3. Their "lost" is specific.**
In class, a classmate asked me to define "lost." Robert's version for Disco Elysium is concrete: lost in history, having lost a country that no longer exists. His "lost" has a time, a place and a cause. Mine needs to be just as specific, not a big word anyone can fit into.

**4. Failure was written into the work.**
A failed novel, being too poor to have a place to live, feeling at nearly 30 that he had nothing but the respect of his friends: all of this became Disco Elysium's temperament, and the hero is himself a failure. In my memory list I wrote "mediocrity is a sin." Disco Elysium does the opposite: it takes failure seriously and gives it dignity. That may be what draws me to it most.

**5. Environments shape games.**
This was the last thing in my notes. They turned their crumbling studio straight into the game's locations. That matters for my next exploration (the room): maybe I don't have to design my protagonist's room from nothing. I can start from places I've lived in and spent time in.

**6. Smaller scope brought out the core.**
Disco Elysium was forced to shrink: no money for combat, so dice and dialogue; a story too big, so one corner of a city. Even its most famous feature, the talking skills, appeared while writing the first dialogue rather than being planned. For me that means two things. Before May I should make something small and complete. And the core mechanic may only show up while I write, so I should write a one-page scene early, test it on paper, and then bring it into Unity.
